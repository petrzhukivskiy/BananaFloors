Beaverton homes by era

Much of Beaverton was built out from the 1950s through the 1980s, and neighborhoods such as Vose, Highland, Five Oaks and Central Beaverton have many ranches and split-levels from that period. Those homes often have hardwood hidden under carpet in older sections, sheet vinyl in kitchens, and a mix of materials added during past remodels.

Hillside neighborhoods like Sexton Mountain and Murrayhill were largely built in the 1980s and 1990s, with two-story layouts, family rooms and carpeted stairs. On the south and west edges, newer townhomes and houses around South Cooper Mountain bring open plans and builder-grade finishes that owners often upgrade. Nearby unincorporated communities such as Cedar Hills, Cedar Mill and Aloha share many of the same housing types.

Plan a flooring replacement

In a replacement, the existing floor tells you a lot. Before we quote, it helps to know what is on the floor now and what might be underneath it:

  • Carpet over hardwood: in some mid-century homes, original oak is hidden under carpet. Look under a floor vent or in a closet corner before you plan; sound oak can often be refinished (hardwood floor refinishing)
  • Layered vinyl: kitchens may have more than one layer of vinyl. Removal gives a flatter, lower floor at doorways (removal)
  • Squeaks: common in older two-story homes and best fixed while the subfloor is exposed (subfloor preparation)
  • Stairs: carpeted stairs in two-story homes often need a matching update (stair flooring)

Apartments, townhomes and rentals

Beaverton has a large rental market and many townhome communities. For rental owners and property managers, durable LVP in kitchens and living areas and stain-resistant carpet in bedrooms make turnovers easier; see our rental flooring page. For townhomes and condos with shared walls, check HOA rules for sound-rated underlayment before choosing a hard floor.

Open living areas and one continuous floor

Many Beaverton homeowners want to replace a patchwork of carpet, vinyl and older laminate with one continuous floor across the main level. Running one product from the entry through the kitchen and living areas makes the house feel larger and simplifies cleaning.

A continuous floor needs planning. Floating floors have maximum run lengths and may need a transition in a doorway, kitchen islands and cabinets affect where the floor goes, and the height has to work at stairs, fireplaces and exterior doors. We map the layout first so transitions, if needed, land in sensible places.

Choosing materials for Beaverton homes

Washington County shares the Portland area's damp winters, and many Beaverton homes are built over crawlspaces. Rigid-core LVP and tile handle kitchens, baths and entries well. engineered wood is a good way to get real wood in open living areas with better seasonal stability than solid boards, and solid hardwood remains a strong choice above grade on wood subfloors. laminate keeps budgets down in bedrooms and offices.

Beaverton flooring questions

Do you install flooring in Beaverton?

Yes. Beaverton is part of our regular service area. Send your project details or call or text (503) 421-1730 for a free preliminary estimate.

I found hardwood under my carpet. Should I keep it?

Possibly. Send photos of the exposed wood and the rooms involved. Whether it is worth keeping depends on its condition and your plans; tell us what you have in mind.

Do you remove old vinyl before installing LVP?

Removal is often the better option, especially with multiple layers. Older vinyl may need testing before it is disturbed. We review the existing floor before confirming the scope.

What flooring is best for a Beaverton rental?

LVP in kitchens, baths and living areas and durable carpet in bedrooms are common choices. Using the same product across units simplifies repairs.
